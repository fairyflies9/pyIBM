# Valveless pumping in a loop of tubing (Jung & Peskin 2001)

A closed loop of tubing, almost rigid except for one straight segment, is pinched
periodically near one end of the flexible segment. There are no valves, yet a net flow goes
round the loop, and its direction depends on the driving frequency. This is the model of
Jung & Peskin, *Two-dimensional simulations of valveless pumping using the immersed boundary
method*, SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 23 (2001) 19-45, the heart-tube case of pyIBM: a tethered
structure in a periodic box, target points that move in time, and the flowmeter diagnostic
of the paper.

## Physics and parameters

The loop is a racetrack (Fig. 1 of the paper): two straight segments of 8 cm at y = 2 and
y = 6 cm joined by semicircles of radius 2 cm about (4, 4) and (12, 4) cm, centreline
length 16 + 4 pi = 28.57 cm (Table 1). Each wall is a closed curve of immersed boundary
points 0.6 cm either side of the centreline, as in the figures (the initial area between the
walls, 34.28 cm^2, is the number quoted under Fig. 4). Every point is tethered to a target
(eq. 2.6): kt = 26000 g/(s^2 cm) on the "almost rigid" part and 900 on the flexible bottom
straight (4 <= x <= 12 cm), plus the paper's term kc (X_{l+1} - 2 X_l + X_{l-1})/ds^2 with
kc = 120 g cm/s^2. Its energy is the sum of the squared segment lengths, so it is written
here as springs of zero rest length and stiffness kc/ds^2 between neighbours. The fluid is
water, rho = 1, mu = 0.01 (CGS), in a 16 cm x 8 cm doubly periodic box.

The drive (section 3.2, Fig. 2): on the left third of the flexible segment, 4 <= x <= 6.67 cm,
the targets of the two walls move apart and together,

    y_target = y0 +/- A(x, t),   A = A0 sin(2 pi t/T) sin(pi (x - 4)/(8/3)),

so with A0 = 0.6 cm the target tube is fully open (walls 2.4 cm apart) a quarter period in
and pinched shut (both walls at y = 2) three quarters in. The physical walls do not follow
the targets closely (Fig. 10-12 of the paper): with the drive on the left third, the largest
target error in the history is 13.6 to 14.8 cells (0.85 to 0.93 cm) at T = 0.325 and 0.21 s
(18.4 cells with the centre third driven), more than the 0.6 cm amplitude itself, and that
is the point of the soft tethers.

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| box | 16 cm x 8 cm, doubly periodic, 256 x 128 cells, h = 1/16 cm (Table 2) |
| points | 3657 (1587 inner wall, 2070 outer), ds = h/4 (the paper: 3654) |
| fluid | rho = 1 g/cm^3, mu = 0.01 g/(cm s) |
| tethers | kt = 26000 (rigid), 900 (flexible) g/(s^2 cm); springs kc/ds^2 = 4.9e5 g/(s^2 cm) |
| drive | A0 = 0.6 cm on 4 <= x <= 6.67 cm; T = 0.325, 0.21, 1.34 s |
| time step | paper: dt = 0.5 h^2 = 0.00195 s (`input2d_pm1996`); midpoint inputs: half of it, 0.00098 s |
| length | 20 cycles (10 for T = 1.34 s); the paper ran 150 s |
| output | 40 history and flowmeter rows per cycle to `viz/<variant>/` |

The paper's scheme is the first-order one of Peskin & McQueen 1996 (upwind advection,
backward Euler): `input2d_pm1996` runs exactly that with the paper's dt. The other inputs
use the second-order midpoint scheme at half the paper's dt, because the pinch pushes fluid
out fast (largest speeds 39 to 49 cm/s in the midpoint runs at T = 0.325 and 0.21 s) and the
paper's dt puts the centred advection above CFL 1: a 20-cycle trial of `input2d` at the
paper's dt reached CFL 1.91, with 71 % of the output rows above 1 (it did not blow up, but
the driver warns). At half the dt the CFL stays below 1 (at most 0.93, at T = 0.21 s).

All inputs set `remove_mean_force = 1`: the targets hold the tube in place, and in a
periodic box the net force they pass to the fluid would otherwise accelerate the box mean.
With this option the solver holds the box-mean velocity at its starting value, zero, in
both schemes. For pm1996 that takes more than subtracting the mean force, because the
first-order upwind advection term is not conservative and has a mean of its own; the solver
removes that as well (`docs/numerics.md`). In every run below the largest |mean_u| and
|mean_v| are at most 2.25e-15 cm/s.

The neighbour springs are stiff: kc/ds^2 = 4.9e5 g/(s^2 cm) gives k dt^2/(rho dx) = 7.5 at the
midpoint dt and 30 at the paper's, and the runs are stable, as the paper's were with the same
stiffness. The driver does not check springs against its tether rule of thumb (along a chain
the stiffest modes sit at the scale of the point spacing, a quarter cell here, and barely
reach the fluid). It checks the targets, which are at 0.4 (midpoint dt) and 1.6 (pm1996),
well under its limit of 10, so these inputs run without a warning.

## How to run

```
python -m pyibm run examples/heart_tube
python -m pyibm run examples/heart_tube --input input2d_ccw
python -m pyibm run examples/heart_tube --input input2d_pm1996
python examples/heart_tube/analyze.py
```

`hooks.py` moves the targets (`update_targets`, a function of absolute time) and is the
paper's flowmeter (`on_output`): at every output it writes to `<output_dir>/flux.csv` the
flux through the vertical section at the middle of the top straight (x = 8 cm, y from 5.4 to
6.6 cm; positive = clockwise = +x) and through the horizontal section at the middle of the
right arc (y = 4 cm, x from 13.4 to 14.6 cm; positive = clockwise = -y), sampling the node
velocity bilinearly, plus the area between the walls (shoelace formula). `analyze.py` prints
the time-averaged flux over the last five cycles at both sections, the cycle-by-cycle means,
the flowmeter range, the area two ways (the largest instantaneous change and the change of
the time-averaged area), the largest CFL number and the box mean for every variant that has
been run, next to the paper's values, and saves `results.png` (flowmeter traces like Fig. 8,
mean flux against period like Fig. 3, area against time like Fig. 5).

## What to expect

The paper's Fig. 3 (A0 = 0.6 cm, time average over 150 s; read from the plotted points in
the vector data of the PDF, to about 0.01): +0.6 cm^2/s at T = 0.325 s (the maximum clockwise
flow; +0.61 at the top section and +0.60 at the right one), -1.6 at T = 0.21 s (the maximum
counterclockwise flow; -1.59 at the top, -1.61 at the right), zero crossing at T = 1.34 s;
-0.0236 cm^2/s with the centre third driven (section 5.2, quoted exactly). Fig. 8 shows the flowmeter over the last five cycles:
-1.1 to +2.5 cm^2/s at T = 0.325 s, -1.1 to +1.35 at 1.34 s, -5.1 to +2.8 at 0.21 s.

Measured here (mean flux over the last five cycles, top section / right section; the area
as the largest instantaneous change and as the change of the time-averaged area, both
relative to 34.28 cm^2):

The CFL numbers are the largest over the output rows.

With the paper's scheme and time step the three cases of its section 5 come out with the
paper's signs: +0.83 cm^2/s clockwise at T = 0.325 s (paper +0.6), -1.17 counterclockwise at
T = 0.21 s (paper -1.6), and -0.02 at T = 1.34 s (the paper's "almost zero"), and the
flowmeter traces have the shape and nearly the range of the three panels of Fig. 8 (-1.1 to
+2.5, -5.1 to +2.8, -1.1 to +1.35). The midpoint runs give the same directions at T = 0.325
and 0.21 s. The two flowmeter sections, which is the paper's volume-conservation check
(Fig. 3), agree to a few per cent where the net flow is large (0.7 to 3 %) and to within
0.03 cm^2/s in the small-flux runs (`input2d_symmetric`, `input2d_zero_pm1996`), where a few
per cent would be meaningless. With the drive moved to the centre third of the flexible
segment the net flow at T = 0.21 s drops to +0.25 cm^2/s over the last five cycles, a
twelfth of the asymmetric drive's -3.0. That is the paper's finding of "almost zero net flow
for the symmetric driving force" (section 5.2) in kind, but not its -0.0236, and it is not
settled: the cycle means stay within 0.06 cm^2/s of zero for twelve cycles and then grow to
+0.39 by cycle 20. We have not run the centre-third drive with the paper's scheme. The
largest instantaneous change of the area between the walls is 0.45 to 1.03 % at T = 0.325
and 0.21 s and 2.2 to 3.3 % at T = 1.34 s. The paper's check is a different measure: Fig. 4
plots the time-averaged area minus the initial area, with a worst case of 0.4196 cm^2,
1.22 %, at T = 0.2 s over 150 s. On that measure our runs stay within 0.6 % of 34.28 cm^2.

The midpoint runs at T = 0.325 and 0.21 s are sensitive to round-off. Two runs of the same
input that differ only in the order of the floating-point operations in the FFT solver
(4e-13 to 1.2e-12 cm^2/s apart in the flowmeter in the second cycle) part ways: their
flowmeter traces differ by more than 0.01 cm^2/s from cycle 10 (T = 0.325 s),
11 (T = 0.21 s) and 13 (centre third) on, and over the last five cycles they give +2.45
against +2.23 cm^2/s at T = 0.325 s, -2.90 against -3.01 at T = 0.21 s, and +0.10 against
+0.25 with the centre third driven. The midpoint numbers in the table carry that
uncertainty. The T = 1.34 s midpoint run gave -0.90 both times.

The magnitudes need a word. With the paper's scheme and time step the net flow at
T = 0.325 s settles by the sixth cycle (cycle means between +0.75 and +0.89 cm^2/s from
then on), 40 % above the paper's 150 s average, and the flowmeter trace has the shape and
range of the top panel of Fig. 8 (a steep rise to +2.9, a shoulder, a dip to -0.8). At
T = 0.21 s it is still creeping up at cycle 20 (-0.97 at cycle 11, -1.22 at cycle 20), so
its last-five-cycle mean, 27 % below the paper's, is not a settled value. With the
second-order midpoint scheme the flow is still spinning up after 20 cycles (cycle means
+0.34, +0.98, +1.43, +1.91, +2.49 at cycles 2, 6, 10, 14, 20 for T = 0.325 s; -0.86, -1.90,
-2.61, -3.25 at cycles 6, 10, 14, 20 for T = 0.21 s), has reached about four times the
paper's value at T = 0.325 s and about twice it at T = 0.21 s, and at T = 1.34 s, where the
paper's curve crosses zero, it gives -0.9 cm^2/s counterclockwise. In pyIBM the two schemes
differ in how much they damp the mean flow. The first-order upwind advection of pm1996 at
h = 1/16 cm and speeds of 20 cm/s adds a numerical diffusion of about u h/2 = 0.6 cm^2/s, and
the mean flow settles in a few cycles. The centred advection of the midpoint scheme adds
none, so its spin-up time is set by the viscous time of the 1.2 cm tube, d^2/nu = 140 s,
far longer than the 6.5 s run here. So pm1996 gives the paper's directions and its zero
crossing, with magnitudes within 40 % of the paper's after 20 cycles (6.5 s and 4.2 s of
its 150 s). Where the midpoint runs would settle we have not found out: that takes minutes
of simulated time (hours of wall time here). The directions at the two resonances, which
are the paper's main point, are the same with both schemes. The pm1996 runs at the paper's
dt reach CFL 0.98 at T = 0.21 s, close to the stability limit of 1 the paper quotes for its
upwind scheme (section 2.2).

Run time: 14 to 30 ms per step on the 256 x 128 grid, depending on the load (the midpoint
run at T = 0.325 s, 6660 steps, took 96 s with three other runs on the machine, the pm1996
run, 3320 steps, 91 s with six); the midpoint inputs take twice the steps of the pm1996
ones. The coarse input runs at about 3 ms per step. The slow tests (ten cycles each at
T = 0.325 s and 0.21 s with the midpoint scheme and at T = 0.325 s with the paper's) took
80, 57 and 24 s with other runs on the machine.

## Files

- `generate.py`: writes the structure files (one per drive setting and grid) and the eight
  inputs; the generated files are committed.
- `hooks.py`: `update_targets` (the drive) and `on_output` (the flowmeter and the area).
- `analyze.py`: the comparison table and `results.png`.
- `tests/examples/test_heart_tube.py`: smoke runs on the coarse and the paper grid, the box
  mean held at zero by the paper's scheme, the committed files against `generate.py`, the
  target law and the flowmeter, the two area measures of `analyze.py`, and slow ten-cycle
  runs at T = 0.325 s (clockwise), T = 0.21 s (counterclockwise) and with the paper's scheme
  (flux within a factor of two of the paper's).
